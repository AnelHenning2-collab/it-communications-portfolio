### FEATURED STORY: What Is Phishing — And Why It Keeps Working

Every month, our security team blocks thousands of phishing attempts before they reach your inbox. But some get through — and when they do, the most important line of defense is you.

Phishing works because it exploits trust, not technology. An email that looks like it is from IT asking you to reset your password. A message that appears to come from your manager asking for urgent help. A notification from a vendor you recognize asking you to click a link.

**Three things to do before you click anything:**

1. **Check the sender address** — not just the display name. The display name can say anything. The actual email address cannot hide.
2. **Hover before you click** — hover your mouse over any link before clicking. The real destination appears at the bottom of your browser.
3. **When in doubt, call** — if an email asks you to take urgent action, call the person directly using a number you already have. Not one from the email.

*Report suspicious emails to: security@company.com*

Your IT account will be created before your first day. On arrival:

### Sample B — Incident Escalation Communication Guide

**Audience:** IT Help Desk staff, Department Managers
**Format:** Decision flowchart + written procedure
**Tools:** Adobe XD · Microsoft Word

---

**IT INCIDENT ESCALATION — COMMUNICATION GUIDE**

When an IT incident occurs, communication must happen at three levels simultaneously:

**Level 1 — Help Desk (0–15 minutes)**
- Log the incident in the ticketing system
- Assign severity level (P1/P2/P3/P4)
- Notify affected user(s) of ticket number and estimated response time

**Level 2 — IT Manager (P1/P2 incidents only — within 30 minutes)**
- Send incident brief via email: what is affected, how many users, current status
- Template: *"[INCIDENT] [Severity] — [System/Service] — [Time Detected] — [Status]"*

**Level 3 — Executive Communication (P1 incidents only — within 60 minutes)**
- IT Manager sends executive brief to CIO and relevant VPs
- Template provided in SharePoint: IT > Incident Communications > Executive Brief Template
- Plain language only — no technical jargon in executive communications

**UNDERSTANDING THE CIA TRIAD**
*What It Means For Your Work*

Every piece of information your organization holds needs three things to be secure. Security professionals call this the CIA Triad.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                         │
│   CONFIDENTIALITY        INTEGRITY        AVAILABILITY  │
│                                                         │
│   Only the right         Information is   Systems work  │
│   people can see         accurate and     when you      │
│   the information        unchanged        need them      │
│                                                         │
│   Think: A locked        Think: A bank    Think: Your   │
│   medical record         statement with   email is      │
│   file                   correct amounts  always on     │
│                                                         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**What this means for you:**

- **Confidentiality** — Do not share passwords, patient data, or financial records with unauthorized people
- **Integrity** — Report anything that looks wrong — a transaction that does not match, a file that seems altered
- **Availability** — Report system outages immediately so IT can restore access fast

### Sample B — HIPAA in Plain English

**Audience:** Healthcare enterprise staff
**Format:** Two-page visual guide
**Tools:** Adobe InDesign

---

**HIPAA — WHAT YOU ACTUALLY NEED TO KNOW**

HIPAA protects patient health information (PHI). If you work in healthcare — or with any healthcare organization — here is what matters:

**The Three Rules in Plain English:**

| Rule | What It Means For You |
|---|---|
| **Privacy Rule** | Patient information can only be shared with authorized people for authorized reasons. When in doubt — do not share. |
| **Security Rule** | Electronic patient data must be protected with passwords, encryption, and access controls. Lock your screen. Use strong passwords. |
| **Breach Notification Rule** | If patient data is exposed — even accidentally — it must be reported immediately. There is no such thing as a small breach. |

**The 6 things that get organizations fined most often:**

1. Sharing PHI by unencrypted email
2. Losing unencrypted devices containing PHI
3. Failing to train workforce on HIPAA annually
4. Not having a signed Business Associate Agreement with vendors
5. Failing to conduct an annual risk assessment
6. Not reporting a breach within 60 days
